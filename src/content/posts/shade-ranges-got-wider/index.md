The shade-count era has been good for the industry's conscience and only partly good for anybody trying to buy foundation. Ranges that used to stop at twelve now run to fifty, and the depth coverage at both ends is genuinely, measurably better than it was a decade ago. What has not improved at the same rate is undertone.

A foundation shade is built from a small number of inorganic pigments: titanium dioxide for opacity and lightness, and iron oxides in red, yellow and black. Everything on the shelf is a ratio of those four. Extending a range downward mostly means increasing pigment load and shifting the iron oxide balance; it does not necessarily mean adding new undertone families. Which is why a fifty-shade range can still offer you exactly two options at your depth, both of them too yellow.

## The three-family shorthand is not enough

Warm, cool and neutral describes a single axis, and skin varies on at least two. Olive is the obvious casualty — a green-grey cast that no amount of adjusting on the warm-cool line accounts for, and which reads as "sallow" only because most ranges have nothing to match it with. Ranges built primarily on red-yellow ratios will always leave olive skin choosing between too pink and too orange.

The second neglected case is depth with cool undertone. Deep shades are frequently formulated warm as a default, on the assumption that warmth reads as healthy. It leaves anyone deep and cool-toned oxidising into a red they did not choose.

## How to test properly

Not on your jaw, and not in a shop. Two rules.

First, test three shades at once, in a stripe, on the centre of the cheek — not the jaw, which is usually a different colour from the rest of the face and frequently a different colour from the neck. Then walk to a window. Retail lighting is engineered to flatter and it will hide a mismatch of half a shade in either direction.

Second, wait. Most liquid foundations shift within twenty minutes as the vehicle evaporates and the pigment settles, and many of them oxidise further over two hours on oily skin. The shade that matched at the counter and looks orange at four o'clock is not a myth, it is the base doing what bases do. Judge it at the two-hour mark or not at all.

## The workaround nobody sells well

Mixing. Two shades from the same range, or a drop of a white or a deep adjuster, solve more problems than a fiftieth shade would — and shade adjusters are the least marketed products in the whole category because they admit the range is incomplete.

If you buy two adjacent shades and blend, you also get the seasonal problem solved. Most people are a genuinely different depth in February and August, and buying twice a year is a worse answer than owning one shade too light and one too dark.

## What actually improved

Depth range, undeniably. Finish options at every depth, which used to be a luxury reserved for the middle of the scale. And, quietly, the willingness of brands to publish undertone in plain language rather than in a code of Ns and Ws and Cs that means something different at every counter.

That last one is the change worth pushing for. Forty shades with honest labels beats sixty with poetry.
