The pitch for Verdal Repair is a reset: one wash that takes your hair back to a baseline, so everything you do afterwards works better. It is a good pitch, and the first half of it is true.

## What it actually is

Sodium lauroyl methyl isethionate and sodium C14-16 olefin sulfonate, in that order, with cocamidopropyl betaine third. That is an efficient anionic system with a co-surfactant to soften it slightly — closer to a clarifying shampoo than to anything a brand would normally label as repair. There is hydrolysed wheat protein and a little panthenol further down, both at levels that will condition marginally and repair nothing.

Nothing in a shampoo repairs hair. Hair is dead keratin; damage to the cuticle is not reversible by anything that rinses off in ninety seconds. Products can smooth, fill, coat and lubricate, all of which improve how damage feels and looks. The word repair, on a bottle, means one of those four things.

## Six weeks, two hair types

On fine hair with a heavy dry-shampoo habit, it was excellent. One wash removed a coating that a gentle sulfate-free formula had been failing to shift for a month, and the root lift afterwards was the best it had been all year. Second wash, same week: too much. The scalp went tight and the ends went straw.

On coarse untreated hair it was less dramatic in both directions — cleaner, rougher, easily rescued by a mask. That is the profile of a fortnightly clarifier, which is what it should have been sold as.

The colour result settled the question. Two washes visibly shifted a copper semi-permanent toward brass. Any formula that lifts colour that fast is not one to use twice a week, whatever the bottle says.

## The instructions are the problem

If Verdal had put "use once every one to two weeks in place of your usual shampoo" on the back, this would be a four-star product with a clear job. Instead the bottle recommends regular use as a full replacement, which sets up exactly the failure the reviews online describe: people wash with it three times a week, their hair gets progressively drier, and they conclude their hair is damaged and needs — reasonably enough, given the name — more repair.

That is not a formulation failure. It is a positioning decision, and it is the one thing about this product we would call dishonest.

## The scent

Worth a paragraph on its own, because it is genuinely lovely and it is why people keep buying it. Dry fig, a dusty cedar, something faintly green underneath. It reads as expensive and it is gone by the time hair is dry, which is the correct behaviour for a scent in a rinse-off product and almost never what happens.

If they released it as an eau de toilette we would review that too.
